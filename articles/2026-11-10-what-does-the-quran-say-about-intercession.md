## Intercession (*Shafāʿa*) in the Quran: What the Text Actually Says

### The question and what the evidence permits

The root *sh-f-3* (ش ف ع) appears **31 times** across the Quran in five grammatical forms: the noun *shafāʿa* (intercession, 13 occurrences), the noun *shafīʿ* (intercessor, 10 occurrences), the verb *yashfaʿu* (he/they intercede, 5 occurrences), the active participle *shāfiʿ* (intercessor, 2 occurrences), and the noun *shafʿ* (even/pair, 1 occurrence). The packet provides 26 verse occurrences to examine. Lane records that the root carries the basic sense of "making a pair" or "coupling," from which the derivative meaning of standing alongside another in petition—intercession—is built (Lane, *Arabic-English Lexicon*, citing S, Mgh, Msb, K, TA).

The evidence permits four clear findings, one significant structural tension, and several questions it cannot resolve.

---

### Finding 1: The dominant claim is denial

The largest single pattern across the packet is **direct rejection of intercession as an operative reality**, particularly for those who relied on it.

At Al-Baqara 2:48 and again at 2:123—two nearly parallel verses—the text warns against "a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted" (Pickthall). Al-Baqara 2:254 adds that the coming day will have "no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession" (Pickthall). These are addressed to believers and frame intercession as something that will simply *not function*.

Al-Anʿām 6:70 describes a soul destroyed by its own deeds: "it hath beside Allah no protecting ally nor intercessor" (Pickthall). Al-Anʿām 6:94 dramatises the moment of return to God, when those who had *claimed* intercessors will see them absent: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you" (Pickthall). Ar-Rūm 30:13 makes the same point: the partners they worshipped will produce no intercessors for them.

Al-Muddaththir 74:48 is unambiguous in its finality: "The mediation of no mediators will avail them then" (Pickthall)—with the word *shāfiʿīn* itself used, so the verse denies effectiveness to those very beings named as intercessors.

The pattern is consistent: for those who expected intercession from beings other than God, it will not arrive.

---

### Finding 2: The text repudiates polytheistic intercession specifically

Several verses connect the expectation of intercession to *shirk* (associating partners with God), and the repudiation operates on that ground.

Al-Anʿām 6:70 and 6:94 describe those who treated created beings as intercessors and "partners." Yūnus 10:18 names the belief explicitly: "They worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors with Allah" (Pickthall)—and the text's rejoinder is a rhetorical challenge: "Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Pickthall).

Az-Zumar 39:43–44 concentrates this argument. Verse 43 asks: "What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence?" (Pickthall). Verse 44 follows immediately: "Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth" (Pickthall). The claim is not that intercession is impossible as a category, but that it belongs entirely to God—making the worship of other intercessors a category error about where authority lies.

---

### Finding 3: A conditional form of intercession is stated

This is where the text becomes structurally more complex. While many verses deny intercession, a cluster of verses preserves a conditional exception.

Al-Baqara 2:255 (Āyat al-Kursī): "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Pickthall). The rhetorical question does not say *no one intercedes ever*; it says no one intercedes *except by His permission*.

Yūnus 10:3: "There is no intercessor save after His permission" (Pickthall).

Tāhā 20:109: "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth" (Pickthall).

Sabā 34:23: "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth" (Pickthall).

An-Najm 53:26: "How many-so-ever be the angels in the heavens, their intercession will avail nothing except after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth" (Pickthall). This verse is notable: it *attributes* intercession to angels but negates its effect without divine permission and divine acceptance of the beneficiary.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28 is the most specific: "they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him" (Pickthall). The referent of "they" in context is beings who know God's knowledge.

Maryam 19:87: "They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord" (Pickthall). Az-Zukhruf 43:86: those who are invoked "possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" (Pickthall).

The consistent structure of this group is: **intercession-as-such is not ruled out, but it is wholly conditional on divine permission, divine acceptance, and some qualifying attribute of the one interceding** (a covenant, 19:87; bearing witness to truth, 43:86; being accepted by God, 21:28; God's leave and satisfaction, 20:109).

---

### Finding 4: The root appears in a mundane, present-world sense

Al-Nisāʾ 4:85 uses *yashfaʿ* without any eschatological context: "Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof" (Pickthall). Here the word describes human advocacy or intervention in ordinary affairs—speaking up for someone in a good or bad cause. Yusuf Ali renders it "recommends and helps." This occurrence shows the root is not confined to the Day of Judgment; it describes a form of advocacy that generates moral consequences for the intervenor.

Al-Fajr 89:3 (*al-shafʿ wa-l-watr*, "the Even and the Odd") uses a cognate form in what both translators render as a numerical or rhythmic oath. This occurrence does not appear to relate to intercession as a practice at all, and the packet does not supply context sufficient to interpret it beyond noting the root connection.

---

### The structural tension

The evidence packet contains a tension the text does not resolve in a single statement:

**On one hand**, verses like Al-Baqara 2:48, 2:123, 2:254, and Al-Muddaththir 74:48 state intercession will not be accepted and will not avail—with no attached condition or exception.

**On the other hand**, verses like Al-Baqara 2:255, Yūnus 10:3, Tāhā 20:109, and An-Najm 53:26 preserve a conditional form in which intercession *can* occur with God's permission.

A reader working from the text alone faces a question of whether these are addressed to different audiences (the polytheists who presumed on false intercessors versus a separate category), or whether the absolute-sounding denials are themselves implicitly conditional. The text does not make this explicit. Al-Aʿrāf 7:53 dramatises the losers' desperate wish for intercessors—"Have we any intercessors, that they may intercede for us?"—and the narrative frame implies their answer is no; but the verse does not explain the principle governing who does and does not receive intercession.

---

### What 39:44 may resolve—partially

Az-Zumar 39:44 says *lillāhi al-shafāʿatu jamīʿan*—"Unto Allah belongeth all intercession" (Pickthall). Read alongside the conditional verses, one possible reading internal to the text is: intercession exists, but it is wholly God's prerogative—He may permit it, and when permitted it occurs on His terms, for whom He chooses, through whom He chooses. The polytheistic error, on this reading, is not believing that intercession exists but believing that beings other than God *own* or *control* it. This reading is consistent with the evidence but cannot be treated as settled; it is an inference, not an explicit statement.

---

### Open questions

This article could not settle the following:

1. **Who are "those who made a covenant with the Most Merciful" (Maryam 19:87) and "those who bear witness to truth" (Az-Zukhruf 43:86)?** The text names the qualifying conditions without identifying the qualified individuals. External sources would be needed to propose candidates, but the packet prohibits introducing them.

2. **What is the referent of "they" in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28?** The verse states they intercede only for whom God accepts and that they fear Him. The packet does not supply the immediately preceding context; identifying the subject class (angels? prophets? some other group?) would require verses not in the packet.

3. **How should the absolute-sounding denials (2:48, 2:123, 74:48) be reconciled with the conditional permissions (2:255, 10:3, 20:109)?** The text does not supply a meta-statement that explains the relationship. This is the central exegetical problem the data raises but cannot resolve.

4. **What does *ʿahd* ("covenant") in Maryam 19:87 refer to?** The term appears without elaboration in this verse; its meaning would require cross-referencing how *ʿahd* is used elsewhere in the Quran, and those verses are not in the packet.

5. **Al-Fajr 89:3** uses the *shafʿ* form in an oath context. Whether this relates conceptually to intercession or is an entirely distinct use of the root (pairing/evenness) cannot be determined from the packet alone.
