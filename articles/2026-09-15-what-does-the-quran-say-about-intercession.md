## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The question is whether the Quran permits intercession (*shafāʿa*), forbids it, or describes it in a more qualified way. The evidence packet allows a reasonably detailed answer: the text takes multiple, distinguishable positions depending on *whose* intercession is in view, *for whom*, and *under what condition*. Working through the 31 occurrences of root **sh-f-3** reveals a structured argument rather than a single verdict.

---

### 1. The Linguistic Ground

Lane records that the root sh-f-3 carries the core sense of making something paired or even—a "shafīʿ" being one who joins himself to another as an advocate or supporter. Lane's authorities (S, Mgh, Msb, K, TA) also list the derived meaning of accepted intercession (*shaffaʿa*: "I accepted his intercession for him") and the causative of seeking it (*istashshafaʿtu*: "I asked him to make intercession for me"). This cluster of meanings—pairing, advocacy, acceptance—maps consistently onto the Quranic uses. The form **shafʿ** also appears once as the simple noun "the even" (Al-Fajr 89:3), which is almost certainly its non-intercession use and will not be treated further here.

---

### 2. Intercession That Will Simply Not Exist

The starkest statements describe a Day on which intercession is entirely unavailable—no exceptions stated:

> "Guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

This is repeated almost verbatim:

> "And guard against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will compensation be accepted from it, nor will intercession be of use to it; nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:123, Pickthall)

And again in a passage addressed to believers:

> "O ye who believe! spend of that wherewith We have provided you ere a day come when there will be no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession." (Al-Baqara 2:254, Pickthall)

These three verses make no exception. Taken alone, they present intercession as categorically absent on the Day of reckoning. The force of each statement is reinforced by parallelism: no substitute (compensation), no ally (friendship), no intercession—three routes of escape, all closed.

Similarly blunt:

> "The mediation of no mediators will avail them then." (Al-Muddaththir 74:48, Pickthall)

The context here (the surrounding verses describe those who neglected prayer, did not feed the poor, and denied the Day of Judgment) restricts the referent to a particular group, but the statement about intercessors is absolute in phrasing.

---

### 3. Intercession Belongs Entirely to God

The most expansive positive claim about intercession is a reattribution rather than a permission:

> "Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth. And afterward unto Him ye will be brought back." (Az-Zumar 39:44, Pickthall)

This verse does not say intercession will happen; it says that if any intercession exists, its totality belongs to God. The rhetorical move is to deny the claim of third-party intercessors (the previous verse, 39:43, challenges those who take intercessors "other than Allah") by asserting exclusive divine ownership of the concept.

---

### 4. No Intercessor Functions Without Divine Permission

A cluster of verses acknowledges that intercessors *exist* in some sense but operate only under a strict condition: prior divine authorization. This is the most frequently recurring structural statement in the packet:

> "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

> "There is no intercessor save after His permission." (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

> "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth." (Saba 34:23, Pickthall)

> "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

> "And how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth." (An-Najm 53:26, Pickthall)

The last verse is notable because it explicitly names angels as potential intercessors—yet qualifies even their intercession with the same double condition: divine leave, and divine approval of the recipient. The rhetorical weight ("how many angels") suggests the point is to close off the assumption that any quantity of heavenly beings constitutes an independent resource.

Al-Anbiyaa 21:28 makes the same point about an unspecified group of beings: "they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him" (Pickthall). The phrase "they quake for awe of Him" reinforces that even these intercessors are subordinate, not independent advocates.

---

### 5. Those Who Claimed Intercessors Will Find Them Gone

Several verses address people who invested in intercessors—expecting them on the Day—and are disappointed:

> "Now have ye come unto Us solitary as We did create you at the first, and ye have left behind you all that We bestowed upon you, and We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you. Now is the bond between you severed." (Al-Anʿaam 6:94, Pickthall)

> "Now we have no intercessors." (Ash-Shuʿaraa 26:100, Pickthall)—spoken, in context, by those in punishment.

> "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah." (Ar-Room 30:13, Pickthall)

> "They worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors with Allah. Say: Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Yunus 10:18, Pickthall)

These passages treat the claim of independent intercessors as a species of shirk (associating partners with God). The question in 10:18—"Would ye inform Allah of something He knows not?"—turns the logic of intercession back on itself: if the proposed intercessor were truly capable of something God did not already will, that would imply a limit on God's knowledge. The verse rejects this.

> "Or choose they intercessors other than Allah? Say: What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence." (Az-Zumar 39:43, Pickthall)

The disqualification here is practical and epistemic: the proposed intercessors lack both power and reason.

---

### 6. Two Conditions for Intercession to Count

Several verses name specific qualifications on either the intercessor's side or the recipient's side:

**On the intercessor's side:**

> "And those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly." (Az-Zukhruf 43:86, Pickthall)

The qualifying intercessor is characterized by bearing witness to the Truth with knowledge.

**On the recipient's side:**

> "They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord." (Maryam 19:87, Pickthall)

Here intercession is framed as belonging to (or available for) those who have made a covenant (*ʿahd*) with the Most Merciful. The Yusuf Ali rendering for this verse reads "received permission from Most Gracious," but the Pickthall rendering stays closer to the Arabic *ittakhadha ʿinda al-raḥmāni ʿahdan* ("taken a covenant with the Most Merciful"). The nature of this covenant is not defined in this verse.

> "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

Two conditions are stated: leave (*adhina lahu*) and acceptability of speech (*raḍiya lahu qawlan*). The verse does not further specify who meets these conditions.

---

### 7. Wrong-Doers Specifically Excluded

One verse qualifies the denial not universally but with reference to a category:

> "Warn them of the Day of the approaching, when the hearts will be choking the throats. There will be no friend for the wrong-doers, nor any intercessor who will be heard." (Ghafir 40:18, Pickthall)

The phrase "no intercessor who will be heard" (*shafīʿin yuṭāʿ*) differs from absolute denial. It may imply intercessors exist but are simply not obeyed on behalf of wrong-doers—rather than that no intercessors exist at all. This is a subtle but potentially significant distinction.

---

### 8. Intercession in This World

One verse applies the root to present human conduct rather than eschatology:

> "Whoever interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof." (An-Nisaʾ 4:85, Pickthall)

Pickthall and Yusuf Ali render this as "intercede/recommend/intervene" in worldly affairs. The verse establishes moral accountability for the intercessor: a good shafāʿa earns a share of the reward; a bad one earns a share of the burden. This is not about the Day of Judgment but about ordinary human advocacy and influence. It demonstrates that the root has an ethically neutral application in this life and that the Quran treats intercession as a genuine moral act with consequences.

---

### 9. Putting the Pattern Together

| Claim | Verses in packet |
|---|---|
| Intercession simply unavailable on that Day | 2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 74:48 |
| All intercession belongs to God | 39:44 |
| Intercession exists but only by divine permission | 2:255, 10:3, 19:87, 20:109, 21:28, 34:23, 53:26 |
| Proposed third-party intercessors (idols, associates) are disqualified | 6:94, 10:18, 26:100, 30:13, 36:23, 39:43, 43:86 |
| Wrong-doers specifically will not benefit | 6:51, 6:70, 7:53, 40:18 |
| Intercession in this life has moral weight | 4:85 |

The consistent underlying logic appears to be: intercession as an *independent* resource available to humans through figures they choose to venerate is rejected outright. What is not rejected—and is instead carefully conditioned—is intercession that operates wholly within divine sovereignty: permitted by God, acceptable to God, on behalf of those God approves. Whether that conditioned intercession is a meaningful practical category or a rhetorical device that effectively reduces to "God decides and no one else" is a question the text generates but does not resolve from within.

### Open Questions

This article cannot settle the following from the evidence packet:

1. **Who are the intercessors in 2:255, 10:3, 20:109, 21:28, 34:23, and 53:26?** The text specifies they act only with divine permission but does not name them. Angels are mentioned in 53:26; 21:28 refers to beings whose identity is not given in the verse. Whether prophets, righteous humans, or other figures are included cannot be determined from these verses alone.

2. **What constitutes the "covenant with the Lord" in 19:87?** The verse names it as a qualifying condition but defines it nowhere in the packet. Resolving this would require tracing *ʿahd* across the full text.

3. **What does "whose word He accepteth" mean in 20:109?** The criterion *raḍiya lahu qawlan* (acceptable speech/word) is stated but not explained.

4. **How to reconcile the absolute-denial verses (2:48, 2:123, 2:254) with the conditioned-intercession verses (10:3, 20:109, etc.)?** The text presents both. One reading harmonizes them by saying the absolute-denial verses address intercession *from* a soul *for* itself, while the conditioned verses address a different configuration. Another reading sees genuine tension. The packet cannot adjudicate between these interpretations.

5. **To whom does "him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" (43:86) refer?** The phrase qualifies an exception to the general denial of intercession-power, but the referent is unspecified in the verse.

6. **Does 40:18's phrasing ("no intercessor who will be heard") imply a weaker denial than 2:48's absolute formulation, and if so, is this distinction meaningful?** The packet raises the question but cannot answer it.
