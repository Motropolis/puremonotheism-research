## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

### What the evidence permits

The packet contains 31 occurrences of the root *sh-f-ʿ* (شفع) across noun and verb forms. The evidence is sufficient to map a clear structural pattern: the Quran simultaneously denies certain kinds of intercession, insists that all intercession ultimately belongs to God alone, and allows for a conditional, permission-based intercession operating within strict divine limits. These three strands run through the corpus and must each be taken seriously. This article traces all three from the text itself.

Lane records that the root covers "making something a pair," and that derived forms include the noun *shafāʿa* (intercession), *shafīʿ* (intercessor), and the verb *yashfaʿ* (to intercede), as well as the formula "I accepted his intercession for him" (*shaffaʿ*) and "I asked him to make intercession for me" (*istasfaʿ*). Lane's authorities cited include S, Mgh, Msb, and K. These glosses confirm the semantic range but do not settle which Quranic uses carry which weight—only the verses can do that.

---

### 1. Absolute negations of intercession

Several verses state flatly that on a coming Day, no intercession will be accepted, avail, or profit:

> *"Guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped."* (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

An almost identical formulation recurs at (Al-Baqara 2:123), and a parallel appears at (Al-Baqara 2:254), which adds that on that Day there will be "no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession."

The wrongdoers condemned to painful punishment in (Al-An'aam 6:70) "have beside Allah no protecting ally nor intercessor," and even if they offered every ransom it would not be accepted. In (Al-Muddaththir 74:48), those who refused to pray or feed the poor are told: "Then will no intercession of intercessors profit them."

These passages deploy intercession as part of a cluster of things—ransom, friendship, compensation, assistance—that all fail on the Day of reckoning. The rhetorical function is to remove any reliance on intermediaries as a substitute for one's own choices and deeds.

---

### 2. Intercession as belonging exclusively to God

Beyond simple negation, several verses make a proprietorial claim:

> *"Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth. And afterward unto Him ye will be brought back."* (Az-Zumar 39:44, Pickthall)

And from the Throne Verse:

> *"Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?"* (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

(Yunus 10:3) states: "There is no intercessor save after His permission." (As-Sajda 32:4) reinforces that beside God, humans have "not a protecting friend or mediator."

The polemic target becomes explicit in (Yunus 10:18), where people worship things that neither harm nor benefit them and say: "These are our intercessors with Allah." The text responds: "Say: Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" The implied logic is that claiming intercessors with God is tantamount to ascribing to God ignorance of something He does not know—a form of *shirk* (association). This is supported by (Az-Zumar 39:43–44), which pairs the question "Or choose they intercessors other than Allah?"—noting these would-be intercessors "have no power whatever and no intelligence"—with the declaration that intercession belongs entirely to God.

The scene at (Al-An'aam 6:94) makes the eschatological version of this explicit: at the moment of judgment, people come alone as they were created, and God says: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you." The intercession they presumed upon has vanished.

---

### 3. Conditional, permission-based intercession

Alongside the absolute negations and the ownership claim, a distinct group of verses describes an intercession that *can* occur—but only under divine authorization:

> *"Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?"* (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

> *"There is no intercessor save after His permission."* (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

> *"On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth."* (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

> *"No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth."* (Saba 34:23, Pickthall)

> *"And how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth."* (An-Najm 53:26, Pickthall)

> *"They cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him."* (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28, Pickthall)

The pattern here is consistent: some entity (left unspecified in most verses; identified as "angels" in An-Najm 53:26) possesses the capacity to intercede, but this capacity is entirely conditional on (a) divine permission (*idhn*) and (b) divine approval (*riḍā*) of both the intercessor and the one interceded for.

Two verses sharpen the conditions placed on the intercessor's side:

- (Maryam 19:87): "None shall have the power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord." (Pickthall)
- (Az-Zukhruf 43:86): "Those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly." (Pickthall)

The covenant (*ʿahd*) and bearing witness to truth (*shahida bil-ḥaqq*) appear as qualifying conditions, though the text does not elaborate on what those conditions entail in concrete terms.

---

### 4. The lament of those who have no intercessors

Two verses record the speech of those who, on the Day of reckoning, wish they had intercessors:

> *"Have we any intercessors, that they may intercede for us? Or can we be returned, that we may act otherwise than we used to act?"* (Al-A'raaf 7:53, Pickthall)

> *"Now we have no intercessors."* (Ash-Shu'araa 26:100, Pickthall)

These are placed in the mouths of those who "were before forgetful" of the messengers' warnings (Al-A'raaf 7:53). Their wish for intercession is answered only by the observation that "they have lost their souls, and that which they devised hath failed them."

---

### 5. A this-worldly occurrence

One verse deploys the root in a context that is not eschatological at all:

> *"Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof."* (An-Nisaa 4:85, Pickthall)

Here *shafāʿa ḥasana* (good intercession/intervention) and *shafāʿa sayyiʾa* (evil intercession/intervention) refer to acts in the present world, with consequences attached in both directions. This verse does not concern the Day of judgment and indicates that the root *sh-f-ʿ* carries a broader meaning of advocacy or intervention that is not restricted to the afterlife frame.

---

### 6. One occurrence outside the intercession cluster

(Al-Fajr 89:3), which reads "And the Even and the Odd," uses the noun *al-shafʿ* in what Pickthall and Yusuf Ali both render as "the Even"—a mathematical/numerical sense of "pair." Lane records the primary root meaning as "making into a pair," which explains this usage. It is morphologically continuous with the intercession root but semantically distinct in this verse.

---

### Structural tension the text does not resolve

The most significant tension in the packet is between the *absolute* negations (Al-Baqara 2:48, 2:123, 2:254; Al-Muddaththir 74:48) and the *conditional allowance* verses (Al-Baqara 2:255; Yunus 10:3; Taa-Haa 20:109; Saba 34:23; An-Najm 53:26; Al-Anbiyaa 21:28). The two sets could be reconciled by reading the absolute negations as applying specifically to intercession *without divine permission*—i.e., the kind of intercessory relationship the idolaters imagined they had with their deities. That reading is textually plausible: (Yunus 10:18) and (Az-Zumar 39:43–44) directly link unauthorized intercessors to idol-worship. But the evidence packet alone cannot *compel* that reconciliation; it can only make it available.

---

### Open questions

This article cannot settle the following:

1. **Who specifically are the intercessors permitted to intercede?** (An-Najm 53:26) names angels; (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28) uses a third-person plural without naming its referent; (Maryam 19:87) and (Az-Zukhruf 43:86) describe qualifying conditions without identifying the persons. The packet does not contain enough information to determine whether these are the same group or different groups.

2. **For whom can intercession be granted?** (Taa-Haa 20:109) says intercession avails one "whose word He accepteth"; (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28) says "him whom He accepteth"; (Saba 34:23) says "him whom He permitteth." The criteria of God's acceptance and approval are not defined within the verses in the packet.

3. **What does "a covenant with his Lord" (Al-Maryam 19:87) mean?** The text states the condition but does not elaborate. Other verses in the packet do not clarify this phrase.

4. **How to read the absolute negation verses against the conditional-permission verses.** The packet allows a harmonizing reading but does not require it. A reader could also conclude that the absolute negations are themselves absolute, making the conditional verses refer only to a permission that is, in practice, never granted. The text as given in this packet cannot adjudicate between these readings.

5. **The referent of "good intercession" in (An-Nisaa 4:85).** Whether this mundane use of *shafāʿa* is continuous with or discontinuous from the eschatological uses is not addressed by the surrounding verses in the packet.
