## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The evidence packet contains 31 occurrences of the root *sh-f-3* (ش-ف-ع), distributed across noun and verb forms. Taken together, the verses establish a consistent framework: intercession is real as a concept, but its availability is sharply conditioned — most dramatically by divine permission. The text also preserves a counter-pattern that complicates easy conclusions, which this article will not smooth over.

---

### What the Root Covers

Lane records several senses for this root: making something a pair or couple, accepting intercession *for* someone, being granted intercession, and seeking intercession (*اِسْتَشْفَعْتُهُ*). The packet lists forms including *shafāʿah* (intercession, noun, 13 occurrences), *shafīʿ* (intercessor, noun, 10 occurrences), *yashfaʿu* (he intercedes, verb, 5 occurrences), *shāfiʿ* (one who intercedes, 2 occurrences), and *shafʿ* (the even/pair, 1 occurrence, in Al-Fajr 89:3 — an oath formula that appears unconnected to intercession in the judicial or eschatological sense).

The dominant usage — across at least 30 of the 31 occurrences — concerns intercession as mediation between a person and God, especially at the Day of Judgment.

---

### Cluster One: Intercession Rejected or Denied

Several verses announce, without qualification, that intercession will be unavailable or useless for certain people on a specific day.

> "And guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

Al-Baqara 2:123 repeats nearly the same formulation: "nor will intercession be of use to it." Al-Baqara 2:254 warns believers to spend before "a day come when there will be no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession." Al-An'aam 6:70 describes a soul that has "beside Allah no protecting ally nor intercessor." Al-An'aam 6:51 uses similar language for those brought before their Lord.

At Al-Muddaththir 74:48, the denial is total and explicit in its repetition of the root: "The mediation of no mediators will avail them then" (Pickthall). Ash-Shu'araa 26:100 places a cry of despair in the mouths of those in punishment: "Now we have no intercessors."

Al-A'raaf 7:53 shows the wrongdoers at the moment of reckoning asking: "Have we any intercessors, that they may intercede for us?" — and the text answers that "they have lost their souls, and that which they devised hath failed them."

Az-Zumar 39:44 states the foundational principle: "Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth." (Pickthall)

---

### Cluster Two: Intercession Permitted Under Strict Conditions

Against those absolute-sounding denials, another set of verses qualifies: intercession does not occur *except* by divine leave and divine acceptance.

Al-Baqara 2:255 (the Throne Verse) frames this as a rhetorical question that implies the answer is "no one, unless permitted":

> "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Pickthall)

Yunus 10:3 states: "There is no intercessor save after His permission." As-Sajda 32:4 denies any *walī* or *shafīʿ* "beside Him" — which, read alongside 2:255 and 10:3, could mean "apart from His authorisation" rather than an absolute absence of any intercessory figure.

Saba 34:23: "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth." (Pickthall)

Taa-Haa 20:109: "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Pickthall)

An-Najm 53:26 explicitly mentions angels: "how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught *save after* Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth." (Pickthall)

Al-Anbiyaa 21:28 supplies yet another condition: "they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him." Two conditions appear here — divine acceptance of the one interceding, and divine acceptance of the one interceded *for*.

---

### Cluster Three: The Rejection of Unauthorized Intercessors

Several verses address the specific practice of venerating beings *as* intercessors with God, and the text repudiates this sharply.

Yunus 10:18 describes people worshipping objects that "neither hurteth them nor profiteth them" and saying "These are our intercessors with Allah." The text's response: "Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" — framing such claims as attributing knowledge to God that He Himself denies.

Al-An'aam 6:94 depicts the Day of Judgment: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you." The intercessors claimed in this life are simply absent.

Ar-Room 30:13: "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah." Az-Zumar 39:43–44 asks whether people take intercessors "other than Allah" who "have power over nothing and have no intelligence," then immediately declares that all intercession belongs to God.

Az-Zukhruf 43:86 introduces a qualification of its own: "those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, *saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly*." (Pickthall)

Maryam 19:87 similarly: "They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord." (Pickthall)

---

### Cluster Four: Intercession in a Non-Eschatological Context

An-Nisaa 4:85 is the only verse in the packet where *shafāʿah* clearly operates in this-worldly human relations:

> "Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof." (Pickthall)

Here intercession or intervention — putting one's weight behind a cause — generates moral accountability in both directions. This usage is consistent with the root's core sense of joining or pairing oneself to something, and it stands apart from the eschatological cluster.

---

### The Structural Tension

Reading across all occurrences, two sets of statements appear to pull against each other:

**Set A** (e.g., 2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 6:70, 74:48): On that Day, no intercession will be accepted or will avail. These statements are unqualified in their wording.

**Set B** (e.g., 2:255, 10:3, 20:109, 21:28, 34:23, 53:26): Intercession is possible, but only with divine permission and divine acceptance — of both the intercessor and the one interceded for.

One reading resolves the tension by scope: Set A applies to the intercessors falsely claimed in shirk (associating partners with God), while Set B describes a genuine but entirely God-controlled intercession. Ar-Room 30:13, Az-Zumar 39:43–44, and Yunus 10:18 support this reading, since they specifically address invented intercessors. However, the unqualified language of 2:48, 2:123, and 2:254 does not mention idolatry — it addresses *anyone* seeking intercession, including believers. The packet does not resolve this definitively from within its own evidence.

What the packet does establish clearly: whatever intercession exists belongs entirely to God (39:44), operates only within His permission (2:255, 10:3, 34:23), is limited to those He accepts (20:109, 21:28), and the wrongdoers (*ẓālimūn*) specifically will have no intercessor who is heard (40:18).

### Open Questions

1. **Who specifically are the permitted intercessors?** The packet mentions "angels" in An-Najm 53:26 and "him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" in Az-Zukhruf 43:86 and "him who hath made a covenant with his Lord" in Maryam 19:87, but does not identify these figures further. Answering this requires examining other roots and verses not present in this packet.

2. **Does Set A (blanket denials) apply universally or only to those in the idolatry cluster?** The packet's evidence is genuinely ambiguous on this point. Resolving it would require a closer study of the broader addressee contexts of 2:48 and 2:123, which are not supplied here.

3. **What does "a covenant with his Lord" (Maryam 19:87) mean?** No verse in the packet defines *ʿahd* in this context.

4. **Is there a distinction between *shafīʿ* (the standing noun "intercessor") and *shāfiʿ* (the active participle "one who intercedes")?** The packet lists these separately, but the two occurrences of *shāfiʿ* (both at Al-Muddaththir 74:48) appear in a single verse and do not supply enough contrast to answer this from usage alone.

5. **Al-Fajr 89:3** uses *shafʿ* in an oath alongside *watr* (the odd). Whether this use of the root connects thematically to intercession, or is simply the numerical/pairing sense of the root, cannot be answered from the packet.
