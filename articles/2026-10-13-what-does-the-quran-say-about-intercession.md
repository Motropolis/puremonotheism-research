## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The evidence permits a fairly detailed answer. Across 31 occurrences of the root *sh-f-ʿ*, the Quran addresses intercession (*shafāʿa*) from several angles: it flatly denies that intercession will function on a certain day; it reserves any possible intercession entirely under God's authority; it condemns the practice of appointing intercessors alongside God; and it applies the same root to a mundane, this-world sense. These strands coexist but pull in different directions on one key question, which is flagged below.

---

### 1. The Lexical Range of the Root

Lane records the core sense of *shaʿa* as "he made it a pair/couple," with derived forms covering intercession and petition. The form *shafāʿa* (noun, 13 occurrences) is the abstract act of intercession; *shafīʿ* (noun, 10 occurrences) is the intercessor-agent; *yashfaʿu* (verb, 5 occurrences) is the action of interceding; *shāfiʿ* (noun, 2 occurrences) is a participial form meaning "one who intercedes"; and *shafʿ* (1 occurrence) appears as the bare noun "even" (paired). That last form, at (Al-Fajr 89:3) — "By the even and the odd" — belongs to a different semantic register entirely and will not be discussed further, since its connection to intercession as a concept is not established within the packet.

---

### 2. Absolute Negations: A Day When Intercession Will Not Work

The most emphatic cluster of verses frames intercession as simply unavailable on the Day of Judgment, with no qualification attached:

> "Guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

(Al-Baqara 2:123) nearly mirrors this wording: "nor will intercession be of use to it." (Al-Baqara 2:254) adds: "before the Day comes when there will be no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession."

These three verses at Al-Baqara share a consistent rhetorical pattern — warning, then a triple or quadruple negation that includes *shafāʿa* alongside ransom, friendship, and mutual aid. The cumulative effect is a strong denial that any social or transactional mechanism will operate on that day.

Similarly blunt is (Al-Muddaththir 74:48): "Then will no intercession of intercessors profit them." Here both the noun *shafāʿa* and the participial noun *shāfiʿīn* ("intercessors") appear together, underscoring the futility.

Those whom the damned hoped would intercede are absent: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you." (Al-An'aam 6:94)

---

### 3. Conditional Permissions: Intercession Only With God's Leave

A second cluster does not deny that intercession ever occurs but insists it is entirely subject to God's permission (*idhn*). This is where the text becomes more nuanced:

> "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

> "There is no intercessor save after His permission." (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

> "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

> "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth." (Saba 34:23, Pickthall)

> "And how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth." (An-Najm 53:26, Pickthall)

> "They cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him." (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28, Pickthall)

The recurring formula — "except after His permission," "except for whom He accepts," "except for him whom He permitteth" — structures intercession as an act that is entirely initiated, authorized, and bounded by God. The would-be intercessors (angels are named in 53:26; others are implied elsewhere) act only where God has already decided to grant leave.

Two verses add further conditions on who may hold intercession:

- (Maryam 19:87): "None shall have the power of intercession, but such a one as has received permission from the Most Gracious." (Yusuf Ali)
- (Az-Zukhruf 43:86): "Those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly." (Pickthall)

These verses distinguish between parties who *do* and *do not* possess power to intercede, with truthful witness and divine permission serving as the qualifying criteria.

---

### 4. Rejection of Unauthorized Intercessors

Several verses address the practice of designating other beings as intercessors with God and treat this as a fundamental error:

> "They worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors with Allah. Say: Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Yunus 10:18, Pickthall)

The rhetorical question ("Would ye inform Allah of something He knows not?") implies that the alleged intercessors have no standing God has recognized. The same logic appears at (Az-Zumar 39:43-44):

> "What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence? Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession." (Pickthall)

The declaration *lillāhi l-shafāʿatu jamīʿā* — "unto Allah belongeth all intercession" — is among the most unequivocal statements in the packet. Intercession is not a distributed commodity that certain beings possess; it belongs wholly to God. The beings appointed as intercessors by those who "take their religion for a pastime" (Al-An'aam 6:70) turn out to have no such function: "It hath beside Allah no protecting ally nor intercessor." (Al-An'aam 6:70, Pickthall); and at the moment of judgment: "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah." (Ar-Room 30:13, Pickthall)

The regret of those who placed hope in unauthorized intercessors appears vividly: "Now we have no intercessors" (Ash-Shu'araa 26:100), a lament at the moment the hoped-for intercession fails. Likewise, at (Al-A'raaf 7:53), those who had neglected the message ask at its fulfillment: "Have we any intercessors, that they may intercede for us?" — a question framed as a forlorn wish, not a reality: "They have lost their souls, and that which they devised hath failed them."

---

### 5. Wrong-Doers Specifically Excluded

One verse is unusually precise in excluding a moral category:

> "There will be no friend for the wrong-doers, nor any intercessor who will be heard." (Ghafir 40:18, Pickthall)

The phrase *yuṭāʿ* ("who will be heard/obeyed") is distinctive: it does not say no intercessor will exist, but that no intercessor will be *effective* for the wrong-doers. This suggests the verse assumes some intercessors may exist while denying they can help this particular group.

---

### 6. Intercession in This-World Conduct

One occurrence uses the root in a clearly non-eschatological context:

> "Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof. Allah overseeth all things." (An-Nisaa 4:85, Pickthall)

Here *yashfaʿ shafāʿatan ḥasanatan* ("intercedes/intervenes with a good intercession") and its evil counterpart describe human moral agency in ordinary affairs, not eschatological pleading. This usage is consistent with Lane's recording of the root's basic sense of pairing or adding one's weight to another's matter.

---

### 7. The Central Tension

The two clusters — absolute denial (2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 74:48) and conditional permission (2:255, 10:3, 20:109, 34:23, 53:26, 21:28) — address what appears to be the same day but reach seemingly different conclusions. One way to read the data from within the text: the absolute denials describe intercession as a *transaction between humans independent of God* (the same register as ransom, friendship, bargaining), which will categorically fail; the conditional verses describe what God alone can authorize, which is not a human-initiated transaction but a divine disposition. This reading fits (Az-Zumar 39:44): "unto Allah belongeth all intercession" — meaning the entire category is under divine sovereignty, not human arrangement.

However, this resolution is a pattern inferred from the text; it cannot be confirmed as the intended meaning from within the packet alone. Verses 2:48 and 2:123 simply say "no intercession will be accepted" with no explicit qualifier, and whether that applies only to unauthorized intercession, or to all intercession as a human-initiated act, is not settled by the text provided.

---

### Open Questions

1. **Who specifically is authorized to intercede?** The text mentions angels (53:26) and "he who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" (43:86), but never provides an exhaustive list within the verses available here. Whether prophets or other figures are included cannot be determined from this packet.

2. **What exactly is the "covenant with the Most Gracious" in (Maryam 19:87)?** The verse conditions intercession on having made a *ʿahd* (covenant/compact) with God, but the packet contains no verse that specifies what that covenant consists of.

3. **How to read the tension between absolute denials and conditional permissions** without importing external sources. The reading proposed above (unauthorized vs. authorized intercession) is coherent but remains an inference from the pattern, not a statement made explicitly in any single verse.

4. **The identity of those who "quake for awe" and intercede in (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28)** is left ambiguous in the verse itself; the packet does not provide surrounding verses that might clarify whether humans, angels, or both are meant.

5. **Whether (An-Nisaa 4:85)** — the this-worldly use of *shafāʿa* — carries implications for how the eschatological uses should be read is a question the packet cannot answer alone.
