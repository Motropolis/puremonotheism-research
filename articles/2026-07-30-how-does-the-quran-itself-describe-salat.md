## What the Quran Says About Salat: A Usage-Based Survey

The question is direct: setting aside all inherited commentary, what does the Quran's own text reveal about *salat* — its character, conditions, purposes, and obligations? The evidence packet gives us 99 total occurrences of the root ص-ل-و (s-l-w), with the noun *ṣalāh* (صَلَاة) accounting for 83 of those, the verb *ṣallā* for 12, and related participial forms for the remaining 4. What follows is an argument from that usage. Readers are encouraged to check every reference cited.

---

### 1. The Lexical Ground

Lane records the primary meaning of *ṣallā* as "he prayed, supplicated, petitioned; he performed the divinely-appointed act" (authorities: S, M, K, MF, TA). The same root carries a physical sense: *ṣalā* denotes "the middle of the back; the part that slopes down from the hips; haunches" (K, M, S), and a related form refers to parts on either side of an animal's tail becoming relaxed (T, S, K, TA, Fr). Lane also records *ṣalawtuhu* as "I struck; beat, that part of him" (K, M).

These are linguistic data points about classical Arabic usage. They do not by themselves determine what *ṣalāh* means in any given Quranic verse. That work must come from reading the verses themselves. The physical senses of the root are noted because readers sometimes invoke them in etymological arguments; the packet cannot confirm or deny those arguments on its own.

---

### 2. Salat Is Commanded Across Generations

One of the most striking patterns in the evidence is how consistently *salat* is linked to figures from across the Quran's narrative scope — not just the immediate audience of the text.

- Abraham prays: "Our Lord, that they may establish *al-ṣalāh*" (Ibrahim 14:37), and himself petitions: "My Lord, make me one who establishes *al-ṣalāh*, and also among my offspring" (Ibrahim 14:40).
- The command reaches Moses: "Establish *al-ṣalāh*" alongside instructions about houses of worship (Yunus 10:87).
- Jesus, speaking from the cradle, reports: "He has enjoined upon me *al-ṣalāh* and almsgiving so long as I remain alive" (Maryam 19:31).
- The same is said of Ishmael: "He used to enjoin upon his people *al-ṣalāh* and almsgiving" (Maryam 19:55).
- Prophets collectively were inspired toward it: "We inspired in them the doing of good deeds and the right establishment of *al-ṣalāh*" (Al-Anbiyaa 21:73).
- The covenant with the Children of Israel explicitly includes: "If you establish *al-ṣalāh* and give the zakāh and believe in My messengers..." (Al-Maaida 5:12; cf. Al-Baqara 2:83).

The Quran, in other words, does not present *salat* as an innovation. It appears as a continuous obligation across prophetic lines.

---

### 3. The Dominant Verbal Formula: "Establish" (Aqīmū/Yuqīmūn)

The most frequent grammatical construction around *ṣalāh* in the packet is the verb *aqāma* — "to establish" or "to uphold." This is not the same as merely "performing" or "doing." The form appears in dozens of verses:

> "Who believe in the Unseen, and establish worship, and spend of that We have bestowed upon them." (Al-Baqara 2:3, Pickthall)

> "Establish worship, pay the poor-due, and bow your heads with those who bow." (Al-Baqara 2:43, Pickthall)

> "The believers, men and women, are protecting friends one of another; they enjoin the right and forbid the wrong, and they establish worship and they pay the poor-due." (At-Tawba 9:71, Pickthall)

The verb *aqāma* carries a connotation of maintaining something upright and constant — not a one-time act but a sustained practice. This is reinforced at Taa-Haa 20:132, where the command is "enjoin upon thy people worship, **and be constant therein**" (Pickthall). Constancy is built into the very grammar of the obligation.

---

### 4. Salat Has a Purpose: Remembrance of God

At one verse the purpose of *salat* is stated explicitly:

> "Lo! I, even I, am God. There is no God save Me. So serve Me and establish worship **for My remembrance**." (Taa-Haa 20:14, Pickthall)

This is one of the clearest internal Quranic glosses on *salat*'s function: it is for *dhikr* — remembrance — of God. A connected verse at Hud 11:114 joins the timing of *salat* to its moral function:

> "Establish worship at the two ends of the day and in some watches of the night. Lo! good deeds annul ill-deeds. This is a reminder (*dhikrā*) for the mindful." (Pickthall)

The link between *salat*, *dhikr*, and moral rectification appears again in the negative: the hypocrites "stand up to worship" but "are mindful of Allah but little" (An-Nisaa 4:142, Pickthall). Their *salat* is defective precisely because remembrance is absent from it. And At-Tawba 9:54 describes those whose *salat* is not accepted — they "come not to worship save as idlers" (Pickthall). The inner disposition is explicitly part of the picture.

---

### 5. Salat Has Physical and Bodily Dimensions

Several verses indicate that *salat* involves physical postures — standing, bowing (*rukūʿ*), and prostration (*sujūd*).

Al-Baqara 2:43 includes the command to "bow your heads with those who bow" (Pickthall), using the root *r-k-ʿ* (bowing). Al-Baqara 2:125 commands the purification of the House "for those who go around and those who meditate therein and **those who bow down and prostrate themselves**" (Pickthall), linking *salat*'s location to both *rukūʿ* and *sujūd*.

An-Nisaa 4:102 describes the prayer during conditions of danger, and in doing so mentions *sujūd* (prostrations) as a component: "when they have performed their prostrations let them fall to the rear" (Pickthall). An-Nisaa 4:103 specifies postures of *dhikr* after *salat* — "standing, sitting and reclining" (Pickthall) — which by contrast implies that the *salat* itself involves a more defined set of postures.

Aal-i-Imraan 3:39 describes Zechariah "standing praying in the sanctuary" (*qāʾimun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrāb*), placing *salat* in a specific physical space and bodily position.

The Quran also specifies a *mṣallā* — a "place of worship" or "station for prayer" — at the standing-place of Abraham (Al-Baqara 2:125). The form *muṣallā* (the single occurrence of that nominal form in the packet's count) points to *salat* as something associated with designated locations.

---

### 6. Salat Has Vocal Dimensions

Al-Israa 17:110 addresses the volume of the *salat* directly:

> "Be not loud-voiced in thy worship nor yet silent therein, but follow a way between." (Pickthall)

This presupposes that *salat* involves something spoken — words, utterances, or recitation — at an audible level that can be modulated. The *salat* is not merely internal or silent. Al-Maaida 5:58 mentions a "call to prayer" (*nādaytum ilā al-ṣalāh*), indicating that the assembly itself is announced.

An-Nisaa 4:43 adds a comprehension requirement: "Draw not near unto prayer when ye are drunken, **till ye know that which ye utter**" (Pickthall). What is said in *salat* must be understood by the one saying it.

---

### 7. Salat Requires Ritual Purity

Two verses describe preparatory purification before *salat*:

> "O ye who believe! When ye rise up for prayer, wash your faces, and your hands up to the elbows, and lightly rub your heads and your feet up to the ankles. And if ye are unclean, purify yourselves." (Al-Maaida 5:6, Pickthall)

> "Draw not near unto prayer when ye are drunken… nor when ye are polluted, save when journeying upon the road, till ye have bathed." (An-Nisaa 4:43, Pickthall)

Both verses also permit the use of clean earth (*tayammum*) when water is unavailable. The framing at Al-Maaida 5:6 is explicit about the purpose: "Allah would not place a burden on you, but He would purify you and would perfect His grace upon you" (Pickthall). Ritual purity (*ṭahārah*) before *salat* is therefore presented not as arbitrary formalism but as part of a comprehensive purification.

---

### 8. Salat Has Temporal Structure

Multiple verses fix *salat* to particular times of day:

- "Establish worship at the going down of the sun until the dark of night, and the Qurʾan at dawn. Lo! the Qurʾan at dawn is ever witnessed." (Al-Israa 17:78, Pickthall)
- "Establish worship at the two ends of the day and in some watches of the night." (Hud 11:114, Pickthall)

An-Nisaa 4:103 provides what is perhaps the strongest statement about the time-bound nature of *salat*:

> "Worship at fixed times (*kitāban mawqūtan*) hath been enjoined on the believers." (Pickthall)

The phrase *kitāban mawqūtan* — literally "a timed/appointed writing" — frames *salat* as a scheduled, prescribed obligation, not an open-ended voluntary devotion.

Al-Baqara 2:238 introduces a specific designation: "the midmost prayer" (*al-ṣalāt al-wuṣṭā*), which implies a set of prayers among which one is distinguished by its central position. The verse commands: "Be guardians of your prayers, and of the midmost prayer, and stand up with devotion to Allah" (Pickthall).

---

### 9. Salat Can Be Shortened and Adapted

An-Nisaa 4:101 permits shortening (*qaṣr*) of *salat* under conditions of travel and fear:

> "When ye go forth in the land, it is no sin for you to **curtail worship** if ye fear that those who disbelieve may attack you." (Pickthall)

An-Nisaa 4:102 elaborates a specific arrangement for group prayer in danger: the congregation divides, with one group praying while the other stands guard, then they rotate. The verse includes prostrations as a component of what is completed before the rotation occurs.

An-Nisaa 4:103 describes *dhikr* continuing after *salat* even in conditions of fear — "standing, sitting and reclining" — and then: "when ye are in safety, observe proper worship." This implies degrees of observance shaped by circumstances.

---

### 10. Salat Is Invariably Paired with Zakāh

Across the packet, *salat* and *zakāh* (giving/purification of wealth) appear as a near-constant pair. They appear together in Al-Baqara 2:43, 2:83, 2:110, 2:277; An-Nisaa 4:77, 4:162; Al-Maaida 5:12, 5:55; At-Tawba 9:5, 9:11, 9:18, 9:71; Maryam 19:31, 19:55; Al-Anbiyaa 21:73; and Ibrahim 14:31 — a pattern that holds whether the command is addressed to this community, to previous communities, or placed in the mouths of the prophets themselves. The pairing is not incidental; the text consistently treats inner devotion and outward generosity as inseparable obligations.

---

### 11. Salat and Its Semantic Breadth: The Root Across Contexts

The packet shows the root s-l-w functioning in at least three distinguishable registers within the Quran:

1. **The prescribed ritual act**: the dominant use, as surveyed above.
2. **Blessings/mercy**: Al-Baqara 2:157 uses the plural *ṣalawāt* — "Such are they on whom are *ṣalawāt* from their Lord, and mercy" (Pickthall). Here the term is used for something God sends *upon* people.
3. **The messenger's prayer for others**: At-Tawba 9:103 — "pray for them. Lo! thy prayer (*ṣalawātaka*) is an assuagement for them" (Pickthall). And At-Tawba 9:99 refers to "the *ṣalawāt* of the messenger" as something people hope to receive. At-Tawba 9:84 uses the verb in a negative prohibition: "never pray for one of them who dieth" — here *ṣallā ʿalā* is prayer *on behalf of* another.

These usages are not contradictory but they do show that the root covers territory beyond the ritual act. When the text says God sends *ṣalawāt*, this cannot mean God performs the same act commanded of believers; context distinguishes the uses. Whether these are fundamentally the same concept extended analogically, or distinct senses that happen to share a root, is a question the packet raises but cannot resolve on its own.

There is also one striking use at Al-Anfaal 8:35:

> "And their worship (*ṣalātuhum*) at the House is naught but whistling and hand-clapping." (Pickthall)

Here what the text calls *ṣalāh* is explicitly dismissed as not genuine. The verse does not say they had no *ṣalāh* at all, but that what they performed amounted to noise. This implies the word could be applied to acts that fail to qualify as proper *salat* — which in turn implies *salat* has criteria that can be met or unmet.

---

### 12. Salat as a Marker of Community

Several verses use *salat* as a marker of belonging or identity. Repentance is described as incomplete without it: "if they repent and establish worship and pay the poor-due, then are they your brethren in religion" (At-Tawba 9:11, Pickthall). Abandoning it signals falling away: "there succeeded them a later generation who ruined worship and followed lusts" (Maryam 19:59, Pickthall). Asking for help through it is commanded: "Seek help in patience and prayer; and truly it is hard save for the humble-minded" (Al-Baqara 2:45, Pickthall). Even the enemies of the believers are described as mocking the call to it (Al-Maaida 5:58).

The text also presents *salat* as difficult, but qualifies who finds it so: it is "hard save for the humble-minded (*al-khāshiʿīn*)" (Al-Baqara 2:45). The word *khushūʿ* — often rendered as humility or reverential awe — appears as the quality that makes *salat* accessible rather than burdensome. This connects *salat*'s outer form to an inner disposition, consistent with the condemnations of those who perform it without genuine attention (An-Nisaa 4:142, At-Tawba 9:54).

---

### Summary of What the Text Establishes

Drawing only from the verses in the packet, *salat* as the Quran describes it is:

- A continuous obligation across prophetic history, not unique to one community
- Commanded using the idiom of "establishment" (*iqāmah*) — implying constancy, not mere performance
- Explicitly oriented toward remembrance of God (*dhikr*)
- Time-bound ("at fixed times"), with morning, midday, and night slots referenced
- Involving physical postures (standing, bowing, prostrating) and audible speech
- Preceded by ritual purification (washing specific limbs, or *tayammum* when water is unavailable)
- Capable of being shortened in travel or danger
- Conducted communally as well as individually
- Inseparably paired with economic generosity (*zakāh*)
- Differentiated from worthless imitation by inner qualities of sincerity and attentiveness

### Open Questions

This article cannot settle the following from the evidence packet alone:

1. **How many daily prayers?** The packet references times of prayer — two ends of the day, watches of the night, the period from the sun's decline to dark, dawn — and a "midmost prayer." These references overlap and resist being counted with certainty from the text. Whether they specify two, three, or five distinct prayer times cannot be determined from these verses without external information the packet does not provide.

2. **What words are spoken in salat?** An-Nisaa 4:43 requires that one "know that which ye utter," and Al-Israa 17:110 presupposes audible speech, but no verse in the packet specifies the content of what is said. Whether recitation of particular Quranic text is intended, and which text, cannot be answered from this evidence alone.

3. **The semantic relationship between the three registers of the root.** Why does the Quran use *ṣalāh*/*ṣallā* for the human ritual act, for divine blessings sent upon people, and for the messenger's intercessory prayer? Is there a unifying concept that the text itself makes explicit? The packet does not supply that answer.

4. **The exact postures and their sequence.** That bowing and prostration occur is clear. The order, number, and mechanics of a complete *salat* unit are not described in the verses available here.

5. **The meaning of *ṣalāt al-wuṣṭā* ("the midmost prayer").** The text names it but does not identify it by time or distinguish it in any other way. The packet cannot resolve which prayer this is.

6. **What "curtailing" (*qaṣr*) entails.** An-Nisaa 4:101 permits curtailing *salat* under threat, but the text does not specify what is reduced — number of units, number of prayers, physical elements, or something else.
