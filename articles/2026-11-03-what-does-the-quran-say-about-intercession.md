## What the Quran Says About Intercession (*Shafāʿa*)

The root *sh-f-ʿ* (شفع) occurs 31 times across the Quran, distributed among the noun *shafāʿa* (intercession, 13 times), the noun *shafīʿ* (intercessor, 10 times), the verb *yashfaʿu* (to intercede, 5 times), the active participle *shāfiʿ* (one who intercedes, 2 times), and the noun *shafʿ* (the even/pair, 1 time). The evidence packet contains 26 verse-occurrences bearing on the question. Lane records the basic semantic range as: making something a pair or even number; granting intercession; and seeking intercession on behalf of another. The social picture behind the word is one of a petitioner appealing through an intermediary to a superior — a structure the Quran both presupposes and repeatedly interrogates.

What the text permits us to say clearly: the Quran does not offer a single uniform answer. Instead it presents a cluster of claims that are in real tension with one another, and tracing that tension carefully is more honest than resolving it prematurely.

---

### 1. The flat denials

Several passages declare, with no qualification, that intercession will not occur or will not help on the Day of Judgment.

> "Guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

The identical warning recurs almost word-for-word at Al-Baqara 2:123. At Al-Baqara 2:254, the same eschatological day is described as one in which there will be "no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession."

The context of those who treated their religion as sport reinforces this at Al-Anʿām 6:70: a soul "will find for itself no protector or intercessor except God" — though even God's exception here is framed in terms of the soul's own acts. And Al-Muddaththir 74:48 states flatly: "The mediation of no mediators will avail them then" (Pickthall).

These verses speak of specific groups — those who disbelieve, those who neglect their obligations — and address their fate without any conditional opening for a third party to intervene.

---

### 2. The permission qualifier

A second, overlapping group of passages does not deny intercession absolutely but conditions any possible intercession entirely on God's prior authorisation. The rhetorical structure shifts from flat denial to sovereign restriction.

Al-Baqara 2:255 (the Throne Verse) frames this as a pointed question:

> "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Pickthall)

The question's implied answer is: no one, unless God first permits it. The same structure appears at Yunus 10:3: "There is no intercessor save after His permission" (Pickthall). Saba 34:23 states: "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth" (Pickthall). Tā-Hā 20:109 adds a second condition alongside permission: "whose word He accepteth" (Pickthall). An-Najm 53:26 specifies that even angels in the heavens cannot make intercession avail "save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth" (Pickthall).

The cumulative logic is unambiguous: intercession is not a resource available to created beings independently. Whatever force it might have is derived entirely from God's will. Az-Zumar 39:44 states this most directly: "Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth" (Pickthall).

---

### 3. Intercession claimed by polytheists — and rejected

A third cluster contextualises intercession as an ideology held by those who worship beings other than God, and the Quran challenges that ideology directly.

At Yunus 10:18, people who worship things "that neither hurteth them nor profiteth them" say: "These are our intercessors with Allah." The response is a rhetorical challenge: "Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Pickthall). The claim to have autonomous intercessors is treated as a form of associating partners with God.

Al-Anʿām 6:94 pictures the moment of standing before God, when the presumed intercessors are simply absent: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you. Now is the bond between you severed" (Pickthall).

Ar-Rūm 30:13 is equally direct: "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah" (Pickthall). Az-Zumar 39:43 challenges the very act of taking intercessors apart from God: "What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence?" (Pickthall).

Al-Anʿām 6:51 and 6:70, Yasin 36:23, and As-Sajda 32:4 all pair the denial of an independent *wālī* (protector/ally) with the denial of an independent *shafīʿ* (intercessor), reinforcing that the two categories are functionally linked and both rejected as independent powers.

---

### 4. Who, if anyone, can intercede?

Alongside the denials, two verses describe conditions under which intercession *does* occur — though both are tightly bounded.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28, speaking of those in the heavens (the context implies angelic or exalted beings), says: "they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him" (Pickthall). The interceding parties are themselves in a state of fearful reverence — they are not confident petitioners but awe-struck beings acting only within God's acceptance.

Maryam 19:87 states: "They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord" (Pickthall). The qualifier here — a covenant (*ʿahd*) with God — shifts the focus to the condition of the one for whom intercession might be sought, or possibly to whoever intercedes; the Arabic pronoun is ambiguous about which referent holds the covenant.

Az-Zukhruf 43:86 adds another condition: "saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" (Pickthall). Here the criterion is not lineage, ritual status, or divine favour granted to a third-party intercessor, but bearing witness to truth with knowledge.

---

### 5. Intercession as a human act in this world

One verse applies *shafāʿa* to present human conduct rather than eschatology:

> "Whoever recommends and helps a good cause becomes a partner therein: and whoever recommends and helps an evil cause, shares in its burden." (An-Nisāʾ 4:85, Yusuf Ali)

Pickthall renders "interveneth" rather than "recommends." Here the root functions in a worldly register — advocating for or enabling something, with moral consequence for the advocate. This usage grounds the word's meaning in concrete social action and shows the root was not exclusively eschatological.

---

### 6. Al-Fajr 89:3 — a separate sense

The only occurrence of the nominal form *al-shafʿ* (the even/pair) appears at Al-Fajr 89:3 in an oath: "By the Even and the Odd." This is lexically the same root — Lane records that the primary sense of *shafaʿa* is "to make a pair" — but the verse has no connection to intercession in its context and should not be read as evidence for the theological discussion.

---

### Reading the tensions together

The passage that does the most synthetic work is Az-Zumar 39:44: intercession *belongs* to God in its entirety. This is compatible with both the flat-denial passages (no creature can intercede independently) and the permission-conditional passages (God can permit intercession). The verses denying intercession to the disbelievers and those who assigned God partners are then denying not intercession as such, but the particular kind of intercession those groups imagined — autonomous, independent, effective without God's leave.

What the text does not do is name who specifically receives God's permission to intercede, or specify who exactly holds the "covenant" of Maryam 19:87, or detail what "acceptable word" in Tā-Hā 20:109 entails. The conditions are stated; their content is not filled in within the verses available here.

### Open questions

This article cannot settle the following from the evidence packet alone:

1. **Who holds the "covenant" in Maryam 19:87?** The verse says intercession belongs to "him who hath made a covenant with his Lord." The packet gives no surrounding verses identifying that party.

2. **Who are the interceding beings in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28?** The referent of "they" in "they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth" requires context not supplied here.

3. **The grammar of Maryam 19:87 — subject or object?** Does "him who hath made a covenant" refer to the potential intercessor or to the one being interceded for? Both readings are grammatically possible.

4. **How do the flat denials (2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 74:48) relate to the permission conditionals (10:3, 20:109, 34:23, 53:26)?** The text presents both; this article has proposed a reading that reconciles them through 39:44, but other readings are possible and the packet alone cannot adjudicate between them.

5. **What distinguishes those for whom God grants permission from those for whom He does not?** Az-Zukhruf 43:86 offers "bearing witness to truth" as a criterion; Tā-Hā 20:109 adds "acceptable word"; but neither verse specifies how these conditions are met or assessed.

6. **Whether the Day-of-Judgment intercession framework applies symmetrically to the worldly-intercession verse (4:85)**, or whether they operate in entirely separate registers, cannot be determined without broader study of how the Quran frames present moral agency versus eschatological judgment.
