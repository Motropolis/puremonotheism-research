## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The evidence packet gives us 31 occurrences of the root *sh-f-3* (ش ف ع) across several grammatical forms. The question is answerable from this evidence in broad outline, but two important tensions within the text must be named honestly rather than smoothed over.

---

### The Linguistic Range

Lane records that the root covers making something a pair (*شَفَعَهُ*), accepting intercession (*شَفَّعَ*), seeking intercession (*اِسْتَشْفَعْتُهُ*), and being granted intercession (*تشفّع*). The noun *شَفَاعَة* (shafāʿa, "intercession," 13 occurrences) and the agent noun *شَفِيع* (shafīʿ, "intercessor," 10 occurrences) dominate the packet. One occurrence — *الشَّفْع* at Al-Fajr 89:3 — is an oath "by the Even," a distinct usage that does not bear on the intercession discussion and will not be treated here.

---

### Claim One: Intercession Belongs Entirely to God

The most sweeping statement in the packet is declarative and unqualified:

> *"Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth."* (Az-Zumar 39:44, Pickthall)

This verse does not carve out exceptions. Read on its own, it locates intercession exclusively with God. Several other verses reinforce divine sovereignty over the matter by insisting that no intercessor may act without prior divine permission:

> *"Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?"* (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

> *"There is no intercessor save after His permission."* (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

> *"And how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth."* (An-Najm 53:26, Pickthall)

The pattern is consistent: any intercession that does occur flows from God's permission and approval, not from any independent power of the intercessor. The sovereign agency is always God's.

---

### Claim Two: Intercession by Others Is Condemned as Shirk

A cluster of verses addresses people who treat beings other than God as intercessors. The text does not merely discourage this — it frames it as a form of associating partners with God:

> *"They worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors with Allah. Say: Would ye inform Allah of that which He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?"* (Yunus 10:18, Pickthall)

> *"Or choose they intercessors other than Allah? Say: What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence."* (Az-Zumar 39:43, Pickthall)

At Al-An'aam 6:94 the scene is the Day of Judgment, where those who assumed they had intercessors arrive to find them absent: *"We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you."* (Pickthall) The word translated "share" is *شُرَكَاء* — partners or associates — linking the intercession claim directly to the concept of shirk.

At Al-An'aam 6:51 and 6:70, the paired denial is emphatic: there is no *walī* (protecting ally) **and** no *shafīʿ* (intercessor) *"beside Him."* Those verses address the situation of a soul at the point of reckoning with no outside help.

---

### Claim Three: On That Day, Certain Intercession Will Not Avail

Multiple verses converge on a category of people for whom intercession is simply useless:

> *"And guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it."* (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

> *"On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth."* (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

> *"The mediation of no mediators will avail them then."* (Al-Muddaththir 74:48, Pickthall)

The wrongdoers (al-ẓālimūn) specifically are told they will have *"no friend nor intercessor who will be heard"* (Ghafir 40:18, Pickthall). Al-A'raaf 7:53 dramatises this: those who neglected the message will ask *"Have we any intercessors?"* — but the text immediately responds that *"they have lost their souls."*

---

### Claim Four: A Conditional Exception

Here is where the text creates genuine complexity. Despite the sweeping negations above, several verses appear to allow for intercession under strict divine conditions:

> *"They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord."* (Maryam 19:87, Pickthall)

> *"And they cannot intercede except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him."* (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28, Pickthall)

> *"No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth."* (Saba 34:23, Pickthall)

> *"And those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly."* (Az-Zukhruf 43:86, Pickthall)

These verses share a grammatical structure — broad negation followed by an *illā* ("except") clause — which preserves the divine monopoly while allowing that under God's permission, and only for those God approves, something called intercession may take place. The *who* intercedes and the *who* benefits are both determined by God.

---

### Claim Five: Intercession as an Earthly, Moral Action

An-Nisaa 4:85 stands apart from the eschatological frame of every other occurrence:

> *"Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof."* (Pickthall)

Here *yashfaʿ shafāʿatan ḥasanatan* ("intercedes a good intercession") and *yashfaʿ shafāʿatan sayyiʾatan* ("intercedes an evil intercession") appear to describe human advocacy or intervention in worldly matters — helping or harming a cause — not eschatological petition. This usage does not contradict the Day-of-Judgment passages because it operates in a different register entirely.

---

### Summary of the Pattern

| Category | What the text says |
|---|---|
| Ownership of intercession | Belongs entirely to God (39:44) |
| Prerequisite for any intercession | God's prior permission and approval (2:255; 10:3; 53:26) |
| Intercession claimed for idols | Rejected as shirk; those entities have no power (10:18; 39:43; 6:94) |
| Wrongdoers on the Day | No useful intercession, none that will be heard (40:18; 74:48; 7:53) |
| Conditional exception | Allowed only for those God has approved, by those God permits (19:87; 20:109; 21:28; 34:23) |
| Earthly meaning | Human advocacy in good or bad causes (4:85) |

### Open Questions

This article could not settle the following, because the packet does not provide enough information:

1. **Who, precisely, are the permitted intercessors?** Maryam 19:87 says "him who hath made a covenant with his Lord"; Az-Zukhruf 43:86 says "him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly"; Al-Anbiyaa 21:28 implies heavenly beings who stand in awe of God. The packet does not establish whether these refer to the same category or different ones, nor what the "covenant" consists of.

2. **Who are the recipients of permitted intercession?** Taa-Haa 20:109 specifies God must have "given leave" and "whose word He accepteth." The packet does not specify which people qualify. The relationship between this exception and the flat negations in 2:48 and 2:123 cannot be resolved from these verses alone.

3. **Does Az-Zumar 39:44's absolute statement** ("all intercession belongs to God") mean that even the permitted intercession of 20:109 and 21:28 is ultimately God acting through agents He controls — or does some real secondary agency exist? The packet's verses support both readings without adjudicating between them.

4. **What does the "covenant" (*ʿahd*) in Maryam 19:87 refer to?** The term appears without definition in this context and is not cross-referenced elsewhere in the packet.

5. **What exactly are the conditions described in Saba 34:23's narrative** ("when fear is banished from their hearts")? The setting and participants of that scene are not explained by the verse alone.
