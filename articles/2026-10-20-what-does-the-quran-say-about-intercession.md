## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The evidence packet allows a reasonably detailed answer to this question. The root *sh-f-ʿ* (شفع) occurs 31 times across the Quran in five distinct grammatical forms — noun (شَفَاعَة, *shafāʿa*), agent noun (شَفِيع, *shafīʿ*; شَافِع, *shāfiʿ*), verb (يَشْفَعُ, *yashfaʿ*), and a nominal form (شَفْع, *shafʿ*). Tracing each form across its contexts reveals a coherent but internally nuanced picture: the Quran sharply constrains who may intercede, on what basis, and for whom — while at the same time rejecting a particular popular *use* of the idea that the text treats as a form of idolatry.

---

### The Linguistic Foundation

Lane records that the core semantic field of *sh-f-ʿ* involves making something a pair — adding a unit to create an even number — and by extension, joining oneself to another as an advocate or supporter. From this, the derived senses include "he accepted his intercession for him" and "I asked him to make intercession for me to such a one" (Lane, citing authorities including S, Mgh, Msb, K, and TA). The word *shafʿ* in its primary sense thus means "even" or "paired," and in social usage, to intercede is to pair oneself with another's cause before a third party.

This matters because it grounds the concept in advocacy and pairing — not in automatic divine forgiveness or compelled mercy. The Quran's usage, as we will see, is consistent with this sense of a conditional, socially mediated plea.

---

### Category 1: Intercession Flatly Refused

Several verses deny intercession to specific groups on the Day of Judgment without qualification. Two nearly parallel warnings in Al-Baqara use the term as part of a list of things that will be unavailable:

> "Guard yourselves against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession (*shafāʿa*) be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:48, Pickthall)

> "Guard against a day when no soul will in aught avail another, nor will compensation be accepted from it, nor will intercession (*shafāʿa*) be of use to it; nor will they be helped." (Al-Baqara 2:123, Pickthall)

Al-Baqara 2:254 adds this to a list alongside trading and friendship:

> "…ere a day come when there will be no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession (*shafāʿa*)." (Al-Baqara 2:254, Pickthall)

Al-A'raaf 7:53 portrays those who neglected the message as belatedly wishing for intercessors, a wish that goes unfulfilled:

> "Have we any intercessors (*shufaʿāʾ*), that they may intercede (*yashfaʿū*) for us? Or can we be returned, that we may act otherwise than we used to act? They have lost their souls, and that which they devised hath failed them." (Al-A'raaf 7:53, Pickthall)

Ash-Shu'araa 26:100 records the same regret in stark brevity: "Now we have no intercessors (*shāfiʿīn*)." (Pickthall)

Al-Muddaththir 74:48 closes this group with a similarly unambiguous statement about a described category of people: "The mediation of no mediators (*shāfiʿīn*) will avail them then." (Pickthall)

Taken together, these verses describe a class of people for whom intercession will categorically fail. The texts do not specify a cosmic law that *no one* can intercede for *anyone*; they specify that it will not profit *these* people. The identity of "these people" is established by the surrounding context in each case — those who forgot the message (7:53), those who disbelieved (2:48, 2:123, 2:254). The structure is conditional on who is being described, not absolute.

### Category 2: Intercession Belongs Exclusively to God

Az-Zumar 39:44 makes the strongest positive claim about who *owns* intercession:

> "Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession (*al-shafāʿata jamīʿan*). His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth. And afterward unto Him ye will be brought back." (Az-Zumar 39:44, Pickthall)

The phrase *al-shafāʿatu jamīʿan* — "all intercession," or "intercession in its entirety" — is a comprehensive possessive. Intercession is not a resource distributed among powerful beings; it belongs wholly to God. This verse directly answers the question of ownership.

---

### Category 3: Conditional Intercession — "Only with His Permission"

A cluster of verses does not deny intercession outright but qualifies it with a strict condition: God's permission and/or God's approval. This is where the picture becomes most nuanced.

Al-Baqara 2:255 (the Throne Verse) poses the condition as a rhetorical question:

> "Who is he that intercedeth (*yashfaʿ*) with Him save by His leave (*illā bi-idhnih*)?" (Al-Baqara 2:255, Pickthall)

The expected answer is: no one — meaning that intercession *without* His leave is simply not possible. But the verse does not say intercession *with* His leave is impossible.

Yunus 10:3 makes the same point positively:

> "There is no intercessor (*shafīʿ*) save after His permission (*illā min baʿdi idhnih*)." (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

Taa-Haa 20:109 specifies a double condition — permission *and* acceptable speech:

> "On that day no intercession (*shafāʿa*) availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Taa-Haa 20:109, Pickthall)

Saba 34:23 adds an emotional scene following the same conditional structure:

> "No intercession (*shafāʿa*) availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth. Yet, when fear is banished from their hearts, they say: What was it that your Lord said? They say: The Truth." (Saba 34:23, Pickthall)

An-Najm 53:26 extends the principle even to angels:

> "And how many angels are in the heavens whose intercession (*shafāʿatuhum*) availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth." (An-Najm 53:26, Pickthall)

Al-Anbiyaa 21:28 specifies who angels may intercede *for*:

> "…they cannot intercede (*yashfaʿūna*) except for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him." (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28, Pickthall)

Maryam 19:87 uses the language of covenant:

> "They will have no power of intercession (*shafāʿa*), save him who hath made a covenant (*ʿahdan*) with his Lord." (Maryam 19:87, Pickthall)

Taken as a set, these verses construct a consistent rule: intercession is possible in principle, but every instance requires God's explicit authorization, and it is effective only for those of whom God approves.

---

### Category 4: The Idolatrous Misuse of the Concept

Several verses address people who selected intercessors *without* divine authorization — treating beings other than God as automatic advocates. The Quran frames this as a fundamental theological error.

Yunus 10:18 is the clearest example:

> "They worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors (*shufaʿāʾunā*) with Allah. Say: Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Yunus 10:18, Pickthall)

The logic of the rebuttal is sharp: if these beings were genuine intercessors, God would know of it. The claim that they are intercessors implies God is unaware of a fact in His own creation — which the Quran treats as absurd.

Az-Zumar 39:43 presses the absurdity further:

> "Or choose they intercessors (*shufaʿāʾa*) other than Allah? Say: What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence?" (Az-Zumar 39:43, Pickthall)

Al-An'aam 6:94 dramatizes the Day of Judgment as the moment when these presumed intercessors simply do not show up:

> "…and We behold not with you those your intercessors (*shufaʿāʾakum*), of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you. Now is the bond between you severed…" (Al-An'aam 6:94, Pickthall)

Ar-Room 30:13 confirms the same fate: "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah." (Pickthall)

Az-Zukhruf 43:86 draws a distinction within this category:

> "And those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly." (Az-Zukhruf 43:86, Pickthall)

The exception — "him who beareth witness unto the Truth" — reintroduces the permission-based framework of Category 3.

---

### Category 5: A This-World Use of *Shafāʿa*

An-Nisaa 4:85 uses the verbal root in an evidently this-worldly, social context:

> "Whoso interveneth (*yashfaʿ*) in a good cause (*shafāʿatan ḥasanatan*) will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause (*shafāʿatan sayyiʾatan*) will bear the consequence thereof." (An-Nisaa 4:85, Pickthall)

Here *shafāʿa* describes ordinary human advocacy or intervention on behalf of others — good or bad — and its consequence is moral accountability, not eschatological intercession. This is fully consistent with the Lane lexical sense of joining oneself to another's cause.

---

### Category 6: *Shafʿ* as "Even"

Al-Fajr 89:3 — "By the Even (*al-shafʿ*) and the Odd" — uses the root in its basic numerical sense and does not bear on the question of intercession. It is included here for completeness only.

### The Pattern in Summary

| What the Quran asserts | Supporting verses |
|---|---|
| A day is coming when intercession will not profit certain people | 2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 7:53, 26:100, 74:48 |
| All intercession belongs to God | 39:44 |
| No one intercedes except by God's permission | 2:255, 10:3, 34:23, 53:26 |
| God also requires approval of the person interceded *for* | 20:109, 21:28, 43:86 |
| Claiming unauthorized intercessors is idolatry | 10:18, 39:43, 6:94, 30:13 |
| Wrong-doers specifically will have no intercessor that is heeded | 40:18, 6:51, 6:70, 32:4 |
| A "covenant with the Lord" is linked to holding intercession | 19:87 |
| Intercession in this world carries moral consequences | 4:85 |

The tension worth noting is textual: some verses use language that sounds absolute ("no intercession," 2:48, 2:254), while others explicitly allow for intercession under the condition of God's leave (2:255, 10:3). A careful reading, following the packet, resolves this not as contradiction but as population-specific: the absolute-sounding denials apply to the wrong-doers and disbelievers in view, while the conditional statements describe the general principle. Ghafir 40:18 crystallizes this precisely — the wrong-doers (*ẓālimīn*) specifically "will have no intimate friend nor intercessor who will be heard" — which implies the rule is not universal but depends on who is being addressed.

### Open Questions

This article cannot settle the following from the packet alone:

1. **Who specifically "holds a covenant with the Lord" (19:87)?** The verse uses this as the exception for possessing intercession, but the packet contains no verses defining what such a covenant (*ʿahd*) requires or who qualifies.

2. **Who are the beings described in 21:28 as interceding only for those God approves?** The verse says "they" (*hum*) quake in fear of God. The packet does not include enough surrounding context to identify them from the text alone — this requires the surrounding verses of Al-Anbiyaa.

3. **Does "bearing witness to the Truth knowingly" in 43:86 refer to a category of humans, angels, or both?** The packet cannot answer this without more context from the surrounding verses.

4. **What is the relationship between the "permission" (*idhn*) condition in 2:255/10:3 and the "covenant" (*ʿahd*) condition in 19:87?** Are these the same threshold described differently, or are they distinct? The packet does not contain verses that would bridge them.

5. **Is the word *shafāʿa* in 4:85 semantically continuous with eschatological intercession, or has the Quran bifurcated the term into two distinct uses?** The packet contains no verse that explicitly links the two uses, and the contexts are markedly different.
