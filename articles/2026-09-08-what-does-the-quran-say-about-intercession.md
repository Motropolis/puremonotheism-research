## What Does the Quran Say About Intercession?

The evidence packet supplies 31 occurrences of the root *sh-f-3* (ش ف ع) across five grammatical forms. The question the evidence permits us to address is: what does the Quran itself say about who may intercede, under what conditions, and for whom? The evidence also permits us to ask what the text is *arguing against* when it invokes intercession. Both questions are traceable; we address them in turn.

---

### The Root and Its Range

Lane records the basic semantic field of *sh-f-3*: to make something a pair, to join one thing to another — hence intercession as the act of "joining oneself to" another's cause. Lane also records *shaffaʿa* as "I accepted his intercession for him," pointing to the transactional, triadic structure the root implies: an intercessor, a subject, and an authority who accepts or rejects. All 31 Quranic occurrences operate within that structure.

The packet's 31 occurrences distribute as follows: *shafāʿa* (noun, intercession) — 13 times; *shafīʿ* (noun, intercessor) — 10 times; *yashfaʿu* (verb, to intercede) — 5 times; *shāfiʿ* (active participle) — 2 times; *shafʿ* (the even/pair) — 1 time. The sole occurrence of *shafʿ* (Al-Fajr 89:3) belongs to a series of oaths — "By the even and the odd" — and carries no intercession meaning in context; it is set aside for the remainder of this article.

---

### Intercession Belongs Entirely to God

The single most direct statement on the ownership of intercession appears in Az-Zumar 39:44:

> "Say: Unto Allah belongeth all intercession. His is the Sovereignty of the heavens and the earth. And afterward unto Him ye will be brought back." (Pickthall)

The structure here is absolute: *all* intercession (*al-shafāʿatu jamīʿan*) belongs to God. This verse functions as a kind of axiom against which the other occurrences must be read. It rules out any intercession that is independent of divine ownership or authorization.

This axiom is reinforced by the framing of intercession alongside divine sovereignty over creation. Both Yunus 10:3 and As-Sajda 32:4 pair a description of God creating the heavens and earth and establishing Himself on the Throne with a denial of any independent intercessor or protector:

> "There is no intercessor save after His permission." (Yunus 10:3, Pickthall)

> "Ye have not, beside Him, a protecting friend or mediator." (As-Sajda 32:4, Pickthall)

The pairing of sovereignty language with intercession language is not incidental. The text is making a governance claim: the one who rules creation is the one who determines whether and how intercession occurs.

---

### The Permission Condition

If Az-Zumar 39:44 establishes ownership, a cluster of verses establishes the condition under which any intercession may occur: explicit divine permission (*idhn*). The permission condition appears across multiple passages with enough consistency to constitute a clear Quranic rule.

- **Al-Baqara 2:255** (the Throne Verse): "Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?" (Pickthall)
- **Yunus 10:3**: "There is no intercessor save after His permission."
- **Taa-Haa 20:109**: "On that day no intercession availeth save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave and whose word He accepteth." (Pickthall)
- **Saba 34:23**: "No intercession availeth with Him save for him whom He permitteth." (Pickthall)
- **An-Najm 53:26**: "How many angels are in the heavens whose intercession availeth naught save after Allah giveth leave to whom He chooseth and accepteth." (Pickthall)

Three elements recur across these verses: (1) intercession without permission is not operative; (2) the permission is God's alone to grant; (3) even celestial beings — angels are explicitly named in An-Najm 53:26 — are subject to this condition. The angels intercede "only for him whom He accepteth, and they quake for awe of Him" (Al-Anbiyaa 21:28, Pickthall). The awe language is significant: it signals that even those capable of intercession stand in subordination, not independent authority.

A second condition appears alongside permission in several verses: acceptability (*riḍā*, approval). Taa-Haa 20:109 requires that the one interceded for have a "word acceptable to Him." An-Najm 53:26 requires that the subject be one God "chooseth and accepteth." Al-Anbiyaa 21:28 says intercession is only for "him whom He accepteth." The text does not specify what makes a person's "word acceptable," but the condition is real and distinguishable from mere permission to intercede.

A further condition appears in two verses that are less often grouped with the permission cluster:

- **Maryam 19:87**: "They will have no power of intercession, save him who hath made a covenant with his Lord." (Pickthall)
- **Az-Zukhruf 43:86**: "Those unto whom they cry instead of Him possess no power of intercession, saving him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly." (Pickthall)

Maryam 19:87 introduces a *covenant* (*ʿahd*) with God as a prerequisite; Az-Zukhruf 43:86 introduces bearing witness to the truth with knowledge. Whether these are the same condition differently phrased, or distinct qualifications, the packet cannot settle. Both verses distinguish between those who do and do not possess intercession capacity (*yamliku al-shafāʿa*), suggesting a genuine category difference — not everyone can intercede, even in principle.

### What the Text Argues Against: False Intercessors

A significant portion of the intercession material is polemical in structure. The text repeatedly addresses people who have taken intercessors *beside God* or *instead of Him* — entities treated as partners or mediators who can advocate with God independently.

**Al-An'aam 6:94** delivers this argument in eschatological terms. On the Day of gathering, it will be said: "We behold not with you those your intercessors, of whom ye claimed that they possessed a share in you. Now is the bond between you severed" (Pickthall). The intercessors (*shufahāʾakum*) are explicitly linked to *partners* (*shurakāʾ*) — beings claimed to "have a share" in the worshippers. On that Day, they are absent.

**Yunus 10:18** sharpens the polemic: people "worship beside Allah that which neither hurteth them nor profiteth them, and they say: These are our intercessors with Allah. Say: Would ye inform Allah of that He knoweth not in the heavens or in the earth?" (Pickthall). The rhetorical question implies that claiming unauthorized intercessors is tantamount to claiming God is ignorant of them — a form of associating partners with divine knowledge.

**Az-Zumar 39:43** asks: "Or choose they intercessors other than Allah? Say: What! Even though they have power over nothing and have no intelligence?" (Pickthall). The verse questions whether these intercessors possess the two things intercession requires: capacity and discernment.

**Ar-Room 30:13** states plainly that on the Day, those who had been treated as partners will not intercede: "There will be none to intercede for them of those whom they made equal with Allah." (Pickthall)

**Al-A'raaf 7:53** gives the scene from the other side — those who are now being judged, who had ignored the messengers, will cry: "Have we any intercessors, that they may intercede for us?" (Pickthall). The answer implied by the verse's structure is no. Their prior assumption of intercession — like the claims in 6:94 and 10:18 — has dissolved.

**Ash-Shu'araa 26:100** preserves this cry in direct speech: "Now we have no intercessors." (Pickthall)

The consistent pattern: claims to independent intercession are described as a form of associationism (*shirk*). The text treats "these are our intercessors with God" as a slogan of those who take partners alongside God, not as a legitimate theological position.

---

### The Total Negation Passages

Several verses state flatly that on the coming Day, intercession will not be accepted or will not profit — without exception clauses:

- **Al-Baqara 2:48**: "No soul will in aught avail another, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor will compensation be received from it, nor will they be helped." (Pickthall)
- **Al-Baqara 2:123**: "No soul will in aught avail another, nor will compensation be accepted from it, nor will intercession be of use to it; nor will they be helped." (Pickthall)
- **Al-Baqara 2:254**: "...ere a day come when there will be no trafficking, nor friendship, nor intercession." (Pickthall)
- **Al-Muddaththir 74:48**: "The mediation of no mediators will avail them then." (Pickthall)
- **Ghafir 40:18**: "...there will be no friend for the wrong-doers, nor any intercessor who will be heard." (Pickthall)

These verses are stated in absolute terms. They sit in apparent tension with the permission-conditioned passages (2:255, 10:3, 20:109, etc.) that allow intercession *with divine leave*. There are three possible readings:

1. The total negation verses and the permission verses address *different subjects*: the former addresses intercession for those not accepted by God, the latter for those who are.
2. The total negation verses address the *Day of Judgment* for a specific group (e.g., those who rejected faith — 2:254 closes with "the disbelievers, they are the wrongdoers"), while the permission verses speak more broadly.
3. The tension is real and unresolvable from within the text alone.

The packet does not provide the interpretive resources to choose definitively among these options. The text *does* contain both kinds of statement. What can be said is that the total negation verses share a common framing: they appear alongside warnings about *equal exchange* (*ʿadl*), help (*naṣr*), and protection — all being denied. The point may be rhetorical: none of the mechanisms of human social rescue will operate. Whether this forecloses God-permitted intercession or runs alongside it is a genuine open question.

### Intercession in This-World Contexts

One occurrence of the root stands apart from all the eschatological material. An-Nisaa 4:85 addresses intercession in a present-tense, ethical register:

> "Whoso interveneth in a good cause will have the reward thereof, and whoso interveneth in an evil cause will bear the consequence thereof. Allah overseeth all things." (Pickthall)

Here the noun is *shafāʿa ḥasana* (good intercession/intervention) and *shafāʿa sayyiʾa* (evil intercession/intervention), and the verb *yashfaʿ* carries its basic meaning of joining oneself to another's cause. This usage is ethically symmetric — both good and bad intercession are addressed — and it implies moral accountability for the intercessor. Whether this verse is about human advocacy in ordinary affairs or something else, the packet cannot settle, but its framing is clearly distinct from the eschatological usage.

### Summary of Patterns

| Pattern | Key Verses |
|---|---|
| All intercession belongs to God | 39:44 |
| No intercession without divine permission | 2:255, 10:3, 20:109, 34:23, 53:26 |
| Even angels require permission | 21:28, 53:26 |
| Covenant/truth-bearing as additional conditions | 19:87, 43:86 |
| False intercessors exposed at Judgment | 6:94, 7:53, 10:18, 26:100, 30:13 |
| Absolute negations of intercession | 2:48, 2:123, 2:254, 74:48, 40:18 |
| This-world ethical intercession | 4:85 |

### Open Questions

This article cannot settle the following from the evidence packet:

1. **The tension between absolute negation and conditional permission.** Al-Baqara 2:48, 2:123, and 2:254 deny intercession without exception; Al-Baqara 2:255, Yunus 10:3, and others allow it with God's permission. Whether these address different groups, different types of Day, or are in genuine tension requires evidence beyond what the packet supplies.

2. **The identity of those with the *covenant* in Maryam 19:87.** The text says those who "made a covenant with his Lord" will possess intercession — but the nature of that covenant, and who qualifies, is not specified in the packet.

3. **The identity of "him who beareth witness unto the Truth knowingly" in Az-Zukhruf 43:86.** The verse distinguishes those who possess intercession capacity from those who do not, based on this criterion, but the packet gives no further context.

4. **Whether the permitted intercessors act *for* others or are themselves the beneficiaries.** The grammar of some permission-clauses (e.g., "save him unto whom the Beneficent hath given leave," Taa-Haa 20:109) is ambiguous about whether the permission-holder is the intercessor or the one interceded for.

5. **The relationship between 4:85 (this-world ethical intercession) and the eschatological passages.** Whether the Quran uses a unified concept of *shafāʿa* across both contexts or whether the root carries meaningfully different senses requires analysis beyond what this packet enables.

6. **What it means for wrongdoers specifically** that they will have "no intercessor who will be heard" (Ghafir 40:18) — whether this implies others *do* have such an intercessor, or whether it is a rhetorical device meaning intercession simply will not function.
